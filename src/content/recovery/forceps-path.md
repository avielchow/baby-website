  - key: what-happened
    label: "What a forceps birth means for the body"
    tone: forceps
    intro: >-
      Forceps got Cody here safely when pushing hit a wall — and they leave more to heal
      than an unassisted birth. Naming that honestly is the point of this page: the recovery
      is real, it is normal for it to be harder, and nearly all of it gets better.
    items:
      - title: "More bruising, more stitches — by design, not by accident"
        detail: >-
          An assisted birth carries a higher chance of an episiotomy or a vaginal tear; the
          cut or tear is repaired right after birth with dissolvable stitches. Expect real
          perineal pain and bruising — walking and sitting can genuinely hurt for a while,
          and a few weeks of swelling and soreness while everything heals is the expected
          course, not a complication. Regular painkillers are part of the plan: ibuprofen is
          the preferred choice while breastfeeding, with acetaminophen a good companion.
        sources:
          - label: RCOG
            url: https://www.rcog.org.uk/media/2p4fh2kd/pi-vaginal-birth-final-28042020.pdf
          - label: ACOG
            url: https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/assisted-vaginal-delivery
      - title: "Bleeding: heavier at first, then the same road as anyone"
        detail: >-
          Right after an assisted birth, heavier bleeding is more common. From there the
          lochia should follow the standard arc — bright red and heavy in the first days,
          pink within about a week, white or yellow after about ten days, tapering over four
          to six weeks. The thresholds that mean "call now" are on the main recovery page:
          soaking a pad in an hour, clots bigger than an egg, or bleeding that trends heavier
          instead of lighter.

  - key: comfort
    label: "The comfort protocol that actually works"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "Cold first, sitz baths daily, water instead of wiping"
        detail: >-
          The proven kit: ice or a cold pack on the sore area for 10–20 minutes at a time
          (thin cloth between ice and skin), a sitz bath — 15–20 minutes of shallow warm
          water, up to three times a day and after bowel movements, then pat dry — and a
          peri-bottle of warm water sprayed over the area during and after using the toilet,
          which stings far less than wiping. Witch-hazel pads laid on the sanitary pad help
          some women. Clean with water only — soap and shower gel irritate a healing wound —
          and give the area fresh air when possible.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abn2976
          - label: ACOG
            url: https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/assisted-vaginal-delivery
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/episiotomy-and-perineal-tears/
      - title: "The stitches timeline (and the fear everyone has)"
        detail: >-
          Up to 9 in 10 first-time mothers have some tear, graze, or episiotomy — this club
          is not exclusive. The stitches dissolve on their own in one to two weeks; the tear
          itself is usually healed in about four to six weeks. And the fear nobody says out
          loud: no, a bowel movement will not open the stitches. It won't. Avoiding
          constipation is still the kindest move — lots of water, fruit and fibre, no
          straining, and a stool softener if the doctor or midwife suggests one.

  - key: third-fourth
    label: "If the tear was third- or fourth-degree"
    tone: forceps
    items:
      - title: "The honest numbers, and the honest reassurance"
        detail: >-
          A tear reaching the anal sphincter (third- or fourth-degree, "OASI") happens in
          about 3 in 100 vaginal births overall, and in 8 to 12 in 100 forceps births — it's
          the main reason forceps recovery gets its own page. When it happens, the repair is
          done properly in an operating theatre, with a course of antibiotics, laxatives to
          keep bowel movements easy, regular pain relief, and a physiotherapist appointment
          as standard aftercare. The long-view number worth holding onto: 6 to 8 in 10 women
          have no long-lasting complications after healing.

  - key: bladder-bowel
    label: "Bladder & bowel: the unglamorous chapter"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "The bladder needs watching in the first day"
        detail: >-
          After an assisted birth (especially with an epidural), fully emptying the bladder
          matters — incomplete emptying now can mean longer-term urinary trouble later, which
          is why hospital staff measure output and why a catheter for up to 24 hours is
          routine, not alarming. At home: drink plenty of water (diluted urine stings less on
          healing skin), and tell the midwife or doctor if peeing is genuinely difficult.
        sources:
          - label: RCOG
            url: https://www.rcog.org.uk/media/2p4fh2kd/pi-vaginal-birth-final-28042020.pdf
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/what-happens/forceps-or-vacuum-delivery/
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/your-body/
      - title: "Leaking is common after forceps — and treatable, not a life sentence"
        detail: >-
          Urinary leaking is common after childbirth generally and more common after a
          forceps delivery; leaking wind or stool can happen too, particularly after a
          third/fourth-degree tear. Two things are true at once: this is normal enough that
          nobody should feel alone in it, and it is treatable enough that nobody should
          quietly live with it. Pelvic floor exercises are the first-line fix, physiotherapy
          is the escalation, and "it may go away on its own or need treatment" is the
          official line — meaning: mention it at the checkup, every time it's still true.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/what-happens/forceps-or-vacuum-delivery/
          - label: ACOG
            url: https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/assisted-vaginal-delivery
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/your-post-pregnancy-body/
      - title: "Hemorrhoids: the postscript nobody warned you about"
        detail: >-
          Pushing for hours has a price. The protocol: ice for about 10 minutes, then a warm
          compress for 10–20 minutes; keep stools soft (water, fibre, stool softener if
          advised); and above all don't strain — straining is what makes them worse. They
          shrink as the weeks pass.

  - key: clots
    label: "One extra risk to respect: blood clots"
    tone: flag
    items:
      - title: "Assisted birth + a 36-hour labor = worth taking seriously"
        detail: >-
          Blood clots in pregnancy and the six weeks after birth are uncommon (1–2 in 1,000
          women), but the risk peaks right after delivery and is higher after an assisted
          birth and after very long labors (more than 24 hours) — both boxes ticked here.
          The defense is easy and boring: move a little every day, drink water, wear the
          compression stockings if given, take the heparin injections if they were
          prescribed. The emergency signs: pain, swelling, or discoloration in one calf or
          thigh (DVT) — and for the lungs, sudden trouble breathing, chest pain, or coughing
          blood, which is a 911 call.

  - key: pelvic-floor
    label: "The pelvic floor: the long game"
    tone: forceps
    items:
      - title: "The muscles that did the most deserve the most rehab"
        detail: >-
          The physio leaflets written specifically for instrumental deliveries say it
          plainly: vaginal birth strains the pelvic floor, and forceps make that strain
          worse — which is why leaking, urgency, heaviness, or painful sex show up more
          often on this path. The counter-program starts early: Kegels can begin as soon as
          any catheter is out (they increase blood flow and speed healing), starting with
          two-to-three-second holds and building toward tens. The full technique, doses,
          and the NHS demonstration video are in the practical file on the main recovery
          page. The escalation rule: symptoms still there after several weeks of honest
          daily work = ask for a physiotherapy referral. Not a fact of life — a referral
          question.
