  - key: watch-for
    label: "The watch list"
    tone: flag
    intro: >-
      A recovering mother is busy, exhausted, and often the last person to flag her own
      symptoms. The partner is the monitoring system. These are the specific things worth
      quietly tracking.
    items:
      - title: "The physical numbers a partner can actually see"
        detail: >-
          Bleeding that soaks a pad in an hour, or clots bigger than an egg — call the
          provider right away. Bleeding that stopped getting lighter and turned heavier, or
          large clots after the first day, or discharge that smells wrong — same-day call.
          Any fever, painful urination, or a wound that looks angrier instead of calmer —
          call. Shivery-and-unwell can be a womb infection announcing itself. None of these
          require Mom to be the one who notices; a partner keeping half an eye on the pad
          count is a legitimate clinical instrument.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=uh4197
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.uhs.nhs.uk/Media/UHS-website-2019/Patientinformation/Pregnancyandbirth/Blood-loss-what-to-expect-after-the-birth-of-your-baby-743a-PIL.pdf
      - title: "The mood clock: two weeks"
        detail: >-
          Tearful, overwhelmed, up-and-down in the first two weeks — that's the blues, and
          it fades. The partner's job is noticing when it doesn't: sadness or hopelessness
          most days, losing interest in things she'd normally enjoy, sleep that doesn't
          come even when Cody sleeps, withdrawal, guilt, or "sad, anxious, or hopeless for
          more than a few days" in Alberta's plain words. Alberta's guidance literally
          assigns family the watching job when risk is higher — a 36-hour labor with
          forceps qualifies. Raising it isn't accusing her of anything; it's reading the
          gauge she can't see from inside.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn9653
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=uh4197
      - title: "The one where the partner must act alone"
        detail: >-
          Postpartum psychosis — sudden confusion, mania, hallucinations, or fixed strange
          beliefs, usually in the first two weeks — is rare (about 1 in 1,000) and is the
          single scenario where waiting for Mom to agree isn't an option, because the
          illness itself hides from the person who has it. The NHS says it directly: the
          partner may have to spot the signs and take action. In Alberta that means the
          emergency department or 911, that day.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-partum-psychosis/

            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn9689&lang=en-ca
  - key: support
    label: "The support moves (with receipts)"
    tone: default
    intro: >-
      Postpartum Support International keeps an evidence-informed list of what actually
      helps. It reads like a job description, which is the point.
    items:
      - title: "Do the housework before being asked"
        detail: >-
          PSI's list is verbatim on this: help before they ask. Asking "what can I do?"
          transfers the management load back to the person who's supposed to be resting.
          The advanced version in a multigenerational house: being the traffic controller
          for the grandparents' help too, so Mom never has to be the one negotiating
          boiled-fruit policy from the couch.
        sources:
          - label: PSI
            url: https://postpartum.net/get-help/family/
      - title: "Protect sleep like it's medicine — because it is"
        detail: >-
          Fatigue is a major driver of postpartum mood symptoms; breaks are "a necessity,"
          not a treat. Taking night feedings so Mom banks a real stretch of sleep is the
          single highest-leverage move on this page — the current pumped-bottle night
          shifts are exactly this play. Add the food corollary: regular meals matter
          because low blood sugar reliably produces low mood, and a recovering,
          breastfeeding body burns fuel fast.
        sources:
          - label: PSI
            url: https://postpartum.net/get-help/family/
          - label: PSI
            url: https://postpartum.net/10-ways-to-support-a-loved-one-experiencing-postpartum-depression/
      - title: "Be the visitor bouncer"
        detail: >-
          Alberta's recovery guidance authorizes limiting visitors; PSI's version says to
          respect and enforce boundaries — "no visitors" included — without taking offense.
          Someone has to deliver those decisions to eager relatives, and it should never be
          the person in the mesh underwear. The partner announces the policy, takes the
          heat, and lets Mom be the good cop.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277
          - label: PSI
            url: https://postpartum.net/10-ways-to-support-a-loved-one-experiencing-postpartum-depression/
      - title: "Listen without fixing, reassure on repeat"
        detail: >-
          The script that helps, per PSI: listen without judgment, come home when you said
          you would, and repeat the three sentences that hold everything up — this is not
          your fault; you are not alone; you will get better. They work at 4 a.m. They work
          in week six. They're not filler; for someone whose brain is telling her the
          opposite, they're load-bearing.
        sources:
          - label: PSI
            url: https://postpartum.net/get-help/family/
  - key: partner-health
    label: "The partner's own tank"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "Dads get postpartum depression too — about 1 in 10"
        detail: >-
          This isn't a hypothetical: roughly one in ten fathers experiences postpartum
          depression, and 5–15% develop an anxiety disorder during pregnancy or the first
          year. The NHS flags it plainly: partners can have depression after a baby.
          Running on one to two hours of broken sleep for days — that happened here in week
          one — is exactly the terrain it grows in. The same two-week rule applies to the
          partner's own mood, and the same doors open: the family doctor, HealthLink 811,
          and PSI's helpline (1-800-944-4773, call or text), which has resources
          specifically for dads.
        sources:
          - label: PSI
            url: https://postpartum.net/get-help/help-for-dads/
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-natal-depression/overview/
      - title: "An empty tank helps nobody"
        detail: >-
          The support structure only works if the support structure sleeps, eats, and
          occasionally leaves the house. Trading off with the grandparents isn't weakness —
          it's maintenance on the family's most-used equipment. The five consecutive hours
          of sleep that turned week one around wasn't a luxury; it was the system working
          as designed.
        sources:
          - label: PSI
            url: https://postpartum.net/get-help/family/
