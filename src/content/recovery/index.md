    label: "Call for help — don't wait"
    tone: flag
    intro: >-
      These are the few things that are never "wait and see." Trust the gut feeling:
      HealthLink 811 is free, 24/7, and staffed by nurses; 911 is for emergencies.
    items:
      - title: "Bleeding that crosses the line"
        detail: >-
          Call the doctor or midwife right away for bleeding that soaks one or more pads in
          an hour, clots bigger than an egg, or feeling dizzy, light-headed, or faint. And
          the slower-burning version matters too: bleeding that trends heavier instead of
          lighter in the weeks after birth, or discharge that smells foul, or just feeling
          unwell — that combination points at infection or late hemorrhage and earns a
          same-day call.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277
          - label: RCOG
            url: https://www.rcog.org.uk/for-the-public/browse-our-patient-information/heavy-bleeding-after-birth-postpartum-haemorrhage/
      - title: "The blood-pressure emergency nobody mentions: postpartum preeclampsia"
        detail: >-
          Preeclampsia can appear for the first time up to six weeks AFTER delivery. A
          reading of 140/90 or higher means call the doctor right away. The symptoms to act
          on without a cuff: a severe headache that won't quit, vision changes, sudden
          swelling of the hands or face, pain in the upper-right belly, or trouble
          breathing. A seizure, chest pain, or loss of consciousness is 911, full stop.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abp9712
      - title: "Blood-clot signs: one leg, or the lungs"
        detail: >-
          Watch for pain, swelling, or reddish-purple discoloration in one calf, the back of
          a knee, thigh, or groin — that's the leg-clot picture, and it needs urgent care.
          Sudden unexplained difficulty breathing, chest tightness or pain, or coughing up
          blood is the lung version, and that's a 911 call. The risk is small but peaks
          right after birth, and it runs higher after an assisted delivery and a long labor.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277
          - label: RCOG
            url: https://www.rcog.org.uk/for-the-public/browse-our-patient-information/reducing-the-risk-of-venous-thrombosis-in-pregnancy-and-after-birth/
      - title: "Infection signs: fever plus anything"
        detail: >-
          After birth, any fever is a call-the-provider event — don't wait on a threshold.
          The picture to watch: fever with increasing pain, swelling, warmth, redness or pus
          at the stitches; foul-smelling discharge; or new or worsening belly or pelvic pain
          (the uterus lining itself can get infected, and antibiotics fix it). For the
          breastfeeding version — a hot, painful breast with flu-like chills — see the
          mastitis entry below: 24 hours of home care, then a doctor.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=uh2916
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abn2976
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/episiotomy-and-perineal-tears/
      - title: "The mind is on this list too"
        detail: >-
          Thoughts of harming oneself, the baby, or anyone else are a 911 call — Alberta's
          own guidance, verbatim. Hearing voices, or suicidal thoughts that persist, means
          calling a doctor the same day. And sudden confusion, mania, or beliefs that don't
          track in the first two weeks is the rare psychosis emergency, where family acts on
          Mom's behalf. The full mental-health map — including what's normal and every help
          line — lives on the Mind & mood page.

  - key: first-days
    label: "The first days"
    tone: default
    intro: >-
      Everything in this phase is loud — the bleeding, the soreness, the exhaustion. Almost
      all of it is the body doing exactly what it's supposed to do.
    items:
      - title: "Afterpains: the uterus shrinking back on schedule"
        detail: >-
          Cramps — often sharpest while breastfeeding — are the uterus contracting back
          toward its pre-pregnancy size. They're a good sign doing an uncomfortable job.
          Acetaminophen or ibuprofen handles them, and both are safe while breastfeeding.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277
      - title: "Bleeding: heavy now, and that's expected"
        detail: >-
          The first days bring genuinely heavy bleeding — super-absorbent maternity pads,
          never tampons (they raise infection risk; pads only until the six-week check).
          Clots smaller than a golf ball can be normal in the first days. The line that
          matters: a pad soaked in an hour or clots bigger than an egg — that's the red-flag
          box above.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tw12302
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/your-body/
      - title: "Perineal care starts on day one"
        detail: >-
          Ice packs 10–20 minutes at a time (cloth between ice and skin), warm water from a
          peri-bottle instead of wiping, and pain relief on schedule rather than heroics.
          The complete comfort protocol — sitz baths, witch hazel, the stitches timeline —
          lives on the forceps path page, because after an assisted birth it deserves the
          full treatment.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abn2976
          - label: ACOG
            url: https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/assisted-vaginal-delivery
      - title: "Day 3–5: the milk arrives"
        detail: >-
          Right around when everyone's most tired, both breasts fill hard and sore —
          engorgement, common between days three and five. It's covered properly in the
          feeding section below; the headline is feed often, hand express to comfort, cold
          between feeds, and it settles within days.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277

      - title: "Rest is a medical instruction, not a luxury"
        detail: >-
          Alberta's guidance says it outright: rest every day, nap when the baby naps, have
          another adult in the house for the first days — and it is officially okay to limit
          visitors, who all want to arrive precisely when Mom is at her most tired. Sleep is
          part of the treatment plan for everything else on this page.

  - key: weeks-1-2
    label: "Weeks 1–2"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "The bleeding changes color on schedule"
        detail: >-
          Lochia turns pink within about a week and white or yellow after about ten days,
          and after two weeks the volume should be much lighter. Getting more active can
          temporarily make it heavier — that's fine as long as the color stays brown or
          pinkish. A return to bright red, or heavier-than-before flow, is the signal to
          slow down and, if it persists, call.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tw12302
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.uhs.nhs.uk/Media/UHS-website-2019/Patientinformation/Pregnancyandbirth/Blood-loss-what-to-expect-after-the-birth-of-your-baby-743a-PIL.pdf
      - title: "Stitches quietly retire"
        detail: >-
          Dissolvable stitches go on their own within one to two weeks — no removal
          appointment, no ceremony. Soreness should be trending better week over week;
          sitz baths (15–20 minutes, up to three times a day) remain the best comfort tool
          through this stretch.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abn2976
      - title: "The bathroom logistics: soft, hydrated, unhurried"
        detail: >-
          Constipation is the enemy of every healing tissue involved. Water constantly,
          fruit and fibre daily, zero straining, and a stool softener if the midwife or
          doctor suggests one. Hemorrhoids get ice first (about 10 minutes), then a warm
          compress (10–20 minutes), and shrink over the weeks as long as straining stays
          off the menu.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/your-body/
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tw12302
      - title: "Pelvic floor work can start now — gently"
        detail: >-
          Kegels can begin as soon as any catheter is out: they increase blood flow to the
          healing area and speed it up, starting with two-to-three-second holds. The full
          technique, doses, and the physio escalation path are on the forceps path page —
          after an instrumental delivery this is the single highest-value habit on the
          whole guide.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.royalberkshire.nhs.uk/media/qskgmuti/physio-instrumental-delivery-and-your-pelvic-floor.pdf
      - title: "The blues window — and Alberta checks in"
        detail: >-
          Tearfulness and mood swings peaking around day four and fading within two weeks
          are the baby blues, and they're expected. Anything that lasts past two weeks
          belongs on the Mind & mood page and in a conversation with a professional. Alberta
          helps here: public health reaches out to families with newborns in the early
          weeks, and the first Well Child Clinic visit includes a formal depression
          screening — answer it honestly.
        sources:

  - key: weeks-3-6
    label: "Weeks 3–6"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "The tear finishes healing"
        detail: >-
          A perineal tear or episiotomy is usually healed at four to six weeks, and the
          bleeding tapers out on roughly the same schedule — some spotting up to six weeks
          is within normal. Tampons stay off the list until the postpartum check clears
          them.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abn2976
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/your-body/
      - title: "Sex: healed first, ready second, lubricant third"
        detail: >-
          The medical gate is healing — about four to six weeks, until bleeding has settled
          and tissue has closed (open healing tissue plus lochia is an infection risk). Past
          that, there is no rule and no schedule: readiness is the couple's call, hormones
          make dryness nearly universal (lubricant is the fix, not a verdict), and pain
          means stop, not push through. After a forceps delivery, tenderness lingering
          longer than expected is a checkup topic, not a marriage problem.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277

      - title: "The fertility surprise: three weeks"
        detail: >-
          Pregnancy is possible three weeks after birth — while breastfeeding, and before
          any period returns, because ovulation comes first. Breastfeeding women can ovulate
          as early as week four. Whatever the plan is, it needs to exist before it's needed;
          the contraception details (including what's compatible with breastfeeding) are in
          the practical file below.

      - title: "The 4–6 week checkup: bring a list"
        detail: >-
          Alberta's complete postpartum visit lands at four to six weeks and covers physical
          recovery plus a direct mood check. The province's own suggested question list is
          good: when to expect a period again, which birth control fits, food and drink
          while breastfeeding, which exercise is right, when sex is okay, and breast
          changes. Add the forceps-specific ones: how the tear healed, any leaking (pee,
          wind, or stool — say it plainly), and whether pelvic floor physio makes sense.

  - key: beyond
    label: "Beyond six weeks"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "The tummy gap check at eight weeks"
        detail: >-
          The two vertical stomach muscles separate in pregnancy (about two finger-widths is
          typical) and usually close by the time baby is eight weeks old. The self-check is
          lying on the back, fingers above the belly button, lifting the head slightly. If
          the gap is still obvious at eight weeks, ask the GP — physiotherapy helps, and
          pelvic floor plus deep-stomach work shrinks it. Until then, sit-ups and planks
          stay shelved.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/your-post-pregnancy-body/
      - title: "Higher-impact exercise waits for clearance — and joints stay loose for months"
        detail: >-
          Walking and gentle movement can start whenever Mom feels up to it, but running,
          aerobics, weights, and cycling wait for the six-week check — and after a
          complicated delivery like a forceps birth, the explicit advice is to ask before
          anything strenuous. One under-known fact: ligaments and joints stay lax for months
          after birth, so overstretching injures easily. Start slow; ease off with pain.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/keeping-fit-and-healthy-with-a-baby/

      - title: "When periods come back (it varies wildly, and that's normal)"
        detail: >-
          With bottle or mixed feeding — like the current pumped-milk nights — the first
          period can return as early as five to six weeks. With full breastfeeding
          including nights, three to twelve months is the range, tied to how often baby
          nurses. One useful tell: if lochia stopped weeks ago and fresh red bleeding
          arrives, that's likely the first period, not a problem.

  - key: feeding
    label: "Breasts & feeding recovery"
    tone: default
    intro: >-
      Breastfeeding is baby's food and Mom's physical work — engorgement, nipple pain, and
      mastitis are recovery topics in their own right. One big thing changed in 2022: the
      old "massage it out, apply heat, pump to empty" advice for blocked ducts was reversed.
      Alberta's guidance now follows the new protocol, and so does this page.
    items:
      - title: "Engorgement: the milk arrives with force (days 3–5)"
        detail: >-
          When milk comes in — usually day 3 to 5, sometimes as late as day 9 — both breasts
          can turn hard, swollen, and sore. Feeding often is the best prevention and the best
          treatment; if a breast is too firm for baby to latch, hand express just enough to
          soften it — comfort, not emptying. Warmth is for the few minutes before a feed only
          (3–5 minutes max; longer heat worsens swelling), cold packs 10–20 minutes between
          feeds do the real work, and acetaminophen or ibuprofen are both safe while
          breastfeeding. Kept up, it settles in a few days.

      - title: "Nipple pain: brief tenderness is normal, real soreness is a latch problem"
        detail: >-
          Slight tenderness when baby first latches is common in the early days. Pain beyond
          that — especially past days 3–5, or cracked and bleeding skin — almost always means
          the latch or position needs fixing, and no cream fixes a bad latch. What helps while
          it heals: rub expressed breast milk into the nipple after feeds (it has healing
          properties), a pea-sized bit of ultrapure lanolin for moist wound healing, and
          varying feeding positions. Get help early if skin is cracked or bleeding — broken
          skin is how infection starts.
        sources:

      - title: "The 2022 rethink: there is no 'plug' to massage out"
        detail: >-
          What used to be called a plugged duct is now understood as inflammation narrowing
          the duct — there is no physical plug, and trying to squeeze or massage one out
          injures tissue and makes it worse. The playbook flipped: ice (hourly if you like)
          and ibuprofen, not heat; the lightest skin-sweeping toward the armpit, never deep
          massage or vibrating gadgets; keep feeding on demand but never feed extra or "pump
          to empty" — over-removal drives the inflammation. Sunflower or soy lecithin (5–10 g
          daily) is a reasonable optional add-on. Alberta's patient guidance has adopted all
          of this.

      - title: "Mastitis: the 24-hour rule"
        detail: >-
          Mastitis announces itself: a hot, swollen, painful area of one breast, often with
          burning pain, chills, and feeling suddenly flu-ish. Treat it like a worse version of
          the above — ice, ibuprofen, normal feeding from both sides (the milk stays safe for
          baby; stopping suddenly makes things worse). The rule: if it isn't clearly improving
          within about 24 hours of that care, or there's fever that persists, see a doctor —
          and red streaks on the breast or pus draining mean call the same day. Most
          inflammatory mastitis resolves without antibiotics, but when they're prescribed,
          finish the course.

      - title: "Supply runs on demand — pump what baby takes, not 'until empty'"
        detail: >-
          Milk supply is a feedback loop: the two biggest levers are how often milk is removed
          and how thoroughly. That cuts both ways — skipped removal lowers supply, but extra
          pumping "to empty" after feeds teaches the body to overproduce, which is a major
          risk factor for engorgement and mastitis. For the night bottle-feeds: pumping
          roughly the volume Cody actually takes, on roughly his schedule, keeps the loop
          honest. Signs of oversupply worth noticing: breasts that never feel drained,
          recurring sore lumps, and a baby who coughs and gulps against a fire-hose letdown.
        sources:

      - title: "Hand expression: the skill that solves three problems"
        detail: >-

          a latch, and works anywhere with no gear. The technique: gently massage first, then
          make a "C" with thumb and finger about 2–3 cm behind the base of the nipple, press
          together for a couple of seconds, release, and build a rhythm — compress and release,
          never sliding on the skin — rotating around the breast. The video below is the
          clearest demonstration we found.

  - key: body-life
    label: "Body & life: the practical file"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "Kegels done right (most people do them wrong)"
        detail: >-
          The squeeze: as if holding in a tampon or stopping wind and urine at once — while
          the buttocks and legs stay completely still and breathing continues. A session is
          8–10 slow squeezes (build from 2–3 seconds toward 10, resting 4–5 seconds between)
          plus 10–20 quick pulses; three sessions a day, every day. Two rules: start with
          short holds while things are sore, and never practice on the toilet — stopping
          urine mid-flow as an exercise confuses the bladder and can cause the very leaking
          it's meant to fix.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.royalberkshire.nhs.uk/media/qskgmuti/physio-instrumental-delivery-and-your-pelvic-floor.pdf
        video:
          title: "How to do pelvic floor exercises | NHS"
          url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JFJtUtKQCuM
          channel: "NHS"
      - title: "Pelvic floor physio in Alberta: two doors"
        detail: >-
          If leaking, heaviness, or pain persists after several weeks of consistent Kegels,
          escalate — that's the official threshold, not an overreaction. In Alberta there
          are two routes: private pelvic-health physiotherapists are direct-access (no
          doctor's referral needed, though insurance may want one for coverage), while the
          public hospital pelvic floor clinics (Lois Hole in Edmonton, Foothills in Calgary)
          run on provider referral — so raise it at the six-week check to get in that queue.
          After an instrumental delivery, this is mainstream aftercare, not a boutique
          extra.

      - title: "Contraception that coexists with breastfeeding"
        detail: >-
          The menu, per Canada's obstetricians: the progestin-only mini-pill can start
          immediately and doesn't interfere with milk; other hormonal methods generally wait
          until about four weeks; an IUD can go in right after delivery or at the six-week
          visit. Breastfeeding itself (the LAM method) only counts as contraception while
          all three conditions hold — exclusive breastfeeding, no period yet, baby under six
          months — and the current pumped-bottle nights already bend the first condition, so
          treat LAM as a bonus, not a plan.

        detail: >-
          Home in one to two days, full recovery around six weeks or more. Expect
          lower-belly pain needing medication for one to two weeks and vaginal bleeding for
          several weeks (lochia happens regardless of how baby exited). Walk a little every
          day — it prevents clots, constipation, and pneumonia — but lift nothing heavier
          than the baby and skip strenuous activity for six weeks. Incision care is simple:
          daily warm soapy water, pat dry, no peroxide or alcohol; a pillow hugged over the
          incision makes coughing bearable.

      - title: "The C-section call-now list"
        detail: >-
          Loose stitches or an incision that opens; a wound that turns red, swollen, or
          leaks; heavy vaginal bleeding; severe or worsening pain; a cough or shortness of
          breath; swelling or pain in a lower leg. Same clot and preeclampsia rules as the
          red-flag box above — those apply to every kind of birth.
