  - key: baby-blues
    label: "The first two weeks: baby blues"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "Crying on day four is the norm, not a warning"
        detail: >-
          Most new mothers get some version of the baby blues: mood swings, tearfulness,
          anxiety, irritability, trouble sleeping, poor concentration. It starts in the first
          few days, typically peaks around day four, and eases within two weeks. It needs no
          treatment beyond rest, support, and being fed — it is hormones and sleep debt doing
          exactly what they do.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn7417
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-natal-depression/overview/
      - title: "The two-week line is the line"
        detail: >-
          The single most useful rule on this page: blues that last beyond two weeks, or get
          worse instead of better, may be postpartum depression — and that's the moment to
          mention it to a doctor, midwife, or the public health nurse. Not because something
          is wrong with her, but because PPD is common (up to 15 in 100 mothers), very
          treatable, and doesn't tend to fix itself.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn7417
          - label: AHS
            url: https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/info/Page16138.aspx
          - label: NIMH
            url: https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/perinatal-depression
  - key: ppd
    label: "When it's more than the blues"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "What postpartum depression actually looks like"
        detail: >-
          Persistent sadness or hopelessness, losing pleasure in things, feeling unable to
          cope, changes in sleep and appetite beyond what the baby demands, exhaustion that
          rest doesn't touch, guilt, trouble concentrating — and sometimes difficulty feeling
          bonded to the baby, which is a symptom, not a verdict on anyone's motherhood. It can
          start any time from pregnancy up to a year after birth, though most episodes begin
          in the first four to eight weeks.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-natal-depression/overview/
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn9653
          - label: NIMH
            url: https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/perinatal-depression
      - title: "It gets better with treatment — usually within months"
        detail: >-
          The evidence is genuinely reassuring: with counselling, medication, or both, PPD
          usually improves within three to six months. Antidepressants compatible with
          breastfeeding exist, so treatment doesn't mean stopping nursing. The WHO's larger
          point is worth keeping: treating a mother's depression measurably improves her
          baby's growth and development — getting help is baby care.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-natal-depression/overview/
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn9653
          - label: WHO
            url: https://www.who.int/teams/mental-health-and-substance-use/promotion-prevention/perinatal-mental-health
  - key: anxiety
    label: "Worry that won't switch off"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "Postpartum anxiety is the quieter twin"
        detail: >-
          Some worry after childbirth is universal. Anxiety becomes the diagnosis when the
          worrying can't be stopped: constant dread, racing thoughts, irritability, plus
          physical symptoms — headaches, nausea, stomach pain, being unable to sleep even
          when the baby sleeps. Same two-week rule, same message: it responds well to
          treatment, usually cognitive behavioural therapy, sometimes medication.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/Pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=aco3773
          - label: PSI
            url: https://www.postpartum.net/learn-more/anxiety/
      - title: "Scary intrusive thoughts are anxious, not dangerous"
        detail: >-
          Many new parents get sudden, disturbing mental images about harm coming to the baby
          — including fleeting thoughts of causing it. These intrusive thoughts are anxious in
          nature, not a break from reality, and they don't reflect intentions or character:
          they horrify precisely because they're the opposite of what the person wants. They
          are still worth telling someone about, because they're very treatable — and nobody
          needs a diagnosis to reach out.
        sources:
          - label: PSI
            url: https://www.postpartum.net/learn-more/anxiety/
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn9653
  - key: birth-trauma
    label: "After a hard birth: trauma is real"
    tone: forceps
    intro: >-
      This section exists because of how Cody arrived: a 36-hour labor, an intense final
      hour, and a forceps delivery. That combination sits squarely on the list of births
      that can leave a mark on the mind as well as the body.
    items:
      - title: "A quarter of women call their birth traumatic — the feeling is the fact"
        detail: >-
          At least 25 in 100 women describe their birth as traumatic, and about 4 in 100
          develop PTSD from it. What makes a birth traumatic is the inside view — feeling
          extremely afraid, helpless, or out of control — not how it looked on paper.
          Difficult forceps births are explicitly on the list of causes, alongside emergency
          C-sections and being left in pain. A birth can be "successful" by every chart and
          still have been terrifying to live through.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.kingstonandrichmond.nhs.uk/patients-and-families/patient-leaflets/understanding-post-traumatic-stress-disorder-after-birth
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.ncic.nhs.uk/patients-visitors/patient-information-leaflets/Maternal-Mental-Health
          - label: PSI
            url: https://www.postpartum.net/learn-more/postpartum-post-traumatic-stress-disorder/
      - title: "What to watch for — and what's just the early days"
        detail: >-
          In the first days and weeks, all kinds of thoughts, feelings, and replays are
          completely normal after a difficult birth. The trauma signals worth acting on are
          the persistent ones: flashbacks that replay the birth like a video, nightmares,
          avoiding anything that recalls it (appointments, the hospital, even talking about
          it), being constantly on alert — jumpy, repeatedly checking the baby is safe — or
          feeling numb and cut off. Effective treatments exist (trauma-focused CBT and EMDR),
          and many hospitals offer a birth-debrief service where you walk through the labour
          record with someone who can explain what happened and why.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.kingstonandrichmond.nhs.uk/patients-and-families/patient-leaflets/understanding-post-traumatic-stress-disorder-after-birth
          - label: PSI
            url: https://www.postpartum.net/learn-more/postpartum-post-traumatic-stress-disorder/
  - key: psychosis
    label: "The rare emergency: postpartum psychosis"
    tone: flag
    items:
      - title: "Rare, sudden, and a 911 call — not a wait-and-see"
        detail: >-
          Postpartum psychosis affects about 1 in 1,000 mothers, usually starting suddenly in
          the first two weeks — often within days of birth. The signs: hallucinations
          (hearing, seeing, or sensing things that aren't there), beliefs that feel true but
          can't be (delusions), feeling impossibly "high" or overactive, severe confusion,
          rapid mood swings. Alberta's guidance is unambiguous: this is a psychiatric
          emergency needing immediate hospital care. Critically, the person affected often
          can't tell she's ill — this is the one condition on this page where the family's job
          is to act, not to ask permission.
        sources:
          - label: NHS
            url: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-partum-psychosis/
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn9689&lang=en-ca
  - key: screening
    label: "Screening: nobody has to self-diagnose"
    tone: default
    items:
      - title: "Alberta screens for this — twice"
        detail: >-
          The system is built to catch what parents miss. At the first Well Child Clinic
          visit (the public-health visit where early immunizations happen), Alberta
          administers the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale — a short questionnaire, available
          in eight languages, that flags depression risk. And the 4–6-week postpartum checkup
          includes being asked about mood directly. Answer both honestly; they only work on
          real answers.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/info/Page16138.aspx
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/Pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277
  - key: help
    label: "Who to call in Alberta"
    tone: flag
    intro: >-
      Every number below was verified current in July 2026. Saving two of these in a phone
      tonight costs nothing.
    items:
      - title: "The ladder, from tonight-is-fine to right-now"
        detail: >-
          Health Link 811 — free nurse advice, 24/7, for any "is this normal?" question.
          Mental Health Help Line 1-877-303-2642 — free, confidential, 24/7, staffed by
          mental-health professionals; does crisis support, screening, and referrals.
          9-8-8 — Canada's suicide crisis line, call or text, 24/7. And the family doctor
          or midwife handles everything that can wait for an appointment: Alberta's own
          advice is to book one whenever low mood lasts more than a few weeks.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/811
          - label: AHS
            url: https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/findhealth/Service.aspx?id=6810&serviceAtFacilityID=1047134
          - label: "988"
            url: https://988.ca/
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/Pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=abl1277
      - title: "Two gentler supports worth knowing"
        detail: >-
          Postpartum Support International runs a helpline — call 1-800-944-4773 or text
          "Help" to the same number — with trained volunteers who return every message daily
          (8am–11pm ET; it's support and connection to local resources, not a crisis line).
          And Alberta's Text4Hope program still runs: text HOPEAB to 393939 for three months
          of free daily texts written by mental-health therapists, built on CBT. Passive,
          free, and surprisingly steadying at 4 a.m.
        sources:
          - label: PSI
            url: https://www.postpartum.net/get-help/psi-helpline/
          - label: AHS
            url: https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/amh/Page17019.aspx
      - title: "The 911 thresholds, verbatim"
        detail: >-
          Alberta's guidance draws these lines: call 911 if there is a plan for suicide or an
          inability to stop harming oneself or the baby. Call a doctor now — same day — for
          hearing voices or suicidal thoughts that persist. And any sudden confusion, mania,
          or beliefs that don't track in the first weeks is the psychosis emergency above:
          911 or the emergency department, with the family making the call if Mom can't.
        sources:
          - label: AHS
            url: https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=tn9653
